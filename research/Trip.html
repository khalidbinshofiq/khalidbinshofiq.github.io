Explaining COVID-Induced Trip Reduction Behavior in the United States

This study uses the 2022 National Household Travel Survey to examine why people who reported making fewer trips attributed that reduction to COVID-19 rather than to other reasons. A Random Forest classifier and partial dependence analysis are used to uncover nonlinear relationships across temporal, spatial, socio-economic and transportation dimensions.

Research Question

Trip Reduction Is Not a Single Behavior

Fewer trips can reflect risk avoidance, telework and digital substitution, but they can also reflect unemployment, constrained accessibility or inability to travel. The study therefore focuses on the reported reason for travelling less, not simply whether mobility declined.

Core question: among NHTS respondents who reported taking fewer trips, which temporal, spatial, socio-economic and transportation characteristics were associated with attributing that reduction to COVID-19 rather than to other reasons?

Data & Method

From NHTS Responses to an Explainable Random Forest

The dependent outcome distinguishes COVID-attributed trip reduction from other reported reasons for making fewer trips. Person, household, vehicle and trip records were merged and filtered into a common person-level dataset.

Start from NHTS person-level responses.

STEP 02 4,109 trip reducers

STEP 03 2,389 final records

Merge datasets and remove invalid / redundant entries.

STEP 04 Random Forest

500 trees; multiple train–test splits evaluated.

Technical details: model training and interpretation
The Random Forest classifier was trained iteratively using alternative train–test splits, with the 70:30 split producing the best reported performance. The final configuration used 500 estimators and random state 42. A correlation check showed that the retained predictors were not strongly collinear, with pairwise correlations ranging approximately from −0.34 to 0.38. Partial dependence plots were then used to interpret the marginal direction and nonlinearity of individual predictors while averaging over the remaining covariates.

Spatial Pattern

Coastal and Large Urban Areas Showed Stronger Reduction Tendencies

Regional differences were not uniform. New England and Pacific census divisions showed the strongest modeled tendencies, while central U.S. regions generally showed lower COVID-attributed trip reduction.

Region

New England & Pacific

These divisions showed the highest modeled trip-reduction tendency.

Large metropolitan areas

Especially large areas with heavy rail showed higher COVID-related reductions.

Interpretation

Post-restriction adjustment

2022 reflects residual health-motivated behavior within a transformed mobility environment.

Temporal result: February showed the strongest COVID-attributed trip-reduction signal, followed by a gradual decline through 2022. Month of travel was also the most important variable in the Random Forest feature ranking.
Socio-economic result: age was the strongest socio-economic predictor. Trip-reduction tendency rose through early and middle adulthood, remained relatively high until around age 60, and then declined. Income showed a nonlinear U-shaped pattern, while higher education groups tended to show stronger reduction.
Transportation result: higher regional gas prices, more online deliveries and more frequent rideshare use were associated with stronger trip reduction. Vehicle owners showed lower reduction than non-owners, while home-based workers showed higher reduction and people who drove or travelled for work showed the lowest tendency.
Transport & Public-Health Planning

What the Findings Mean for Future Mobility Shocks

The main planning message is that the ability and willingness to reduce travel are uneven. Emergency mobility policies therefore need to account for geography, work flexibility, digital substitution, socio-economic resources and access to private or shared mobility.

Timing

Match interventions to changing risk periods

Communication and mobility-management measures should respond to temporal variation rather than treating an emergency as behaviorally uniform.

Regional Context

Tailor support geographically

Metropolitan and regional differences suggest that one national mobility message may not generate the same response everywhere.

Equity

Protect people unable to reduce essential travel

Lower flexibility in employment or mobility resources can limit the ability to avoid exposure even when health risks are high.

Digital Access

Support telework and delivery substitution

Digital alternatives can reduce physical travel for some activities, but access to those alternatives is itself uneven.

Mobility Resilience

Maintain safe essential transport

Public and shared transport must remain reliable and safe for travelers who cannot substitute or postpone necessary trips.

Interpretation

Do not equate fewer trips with better accessibility

Trip reduction can signal successful substitution, constrained opportunity, financial hardship or health-risk avoidance.

Responsible Interpretation

Limitations & Research Output

Key limitations
The 2022 NHTS is cross-sectional and cannot establish causal effects or long-term behavioral change. COVID-related trip reduction is self-reported, so reporting bias is possible. The analysis also does not explicitly model changing local COVID policies or infection intensity. Partial dependence therefore describes modeled associations, not causal mechanisms.
